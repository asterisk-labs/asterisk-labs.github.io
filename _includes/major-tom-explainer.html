1

The grid

AI models like square chips of rasters. Mostly. We define a grid, starting from the equator and dividing our planet into strips of 10 km. In each row (one strip), we divide the circumference of that circle of latitude into 10 km segments, to create approximately square cells. There are 4,008 of them at the equator, but only 2,472 of them at 51.9°N. In total, Earth is covered by 5,113,745 cells of Major TOM at 10 km spacing.

2

One cell

We index cells by indicating how many rows up or down (U/D) and how far to the left or right (L/R) a given segment is. For example, you can find the Bonnie Banks o' Loch Lomond if you go 624 rows up from the equator, and move 29 cells to the left of the Greenwich meridian in that row. This leaves us with a code of 624U_29L. You can see that on top of that, we define a 10.68 km footprint for a chip in order to cover the cell in a product with UTM projection. Newer Major TOM products often use 10.56 km chips, centred on the cell, for optimised chunking. The underlying 10.00 km grid remains the same in both cases.

3

Crop and map

This is how a larger (about 110 km on each side) Sentinel-2 product gets cropped into a Major TOM sample. We can do this to any source, in any projection, although it is best when it is in UTM. See 4 examples of Major TOM products over the same cell. Most of them can be easily built using majortom.build functionality in our code.
